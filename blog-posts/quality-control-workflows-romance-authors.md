# My Romance Novel Quality Control System: How I Catch Every Plot Hole Before Readers Do

I used to finish a manuscript, do one light pass, and hit publish. Then I'd wake up to DMs about a timeline that didn't add up, a character eye color that switched, or a suspense clue that never paid off.

Readers notice everything. Especially romance readers, who will read the same scene three times and remember exactly what was promised on page 47.

So I built a quality control system. Not a fancy tool. A workflow. A repeatable set of audits I run before any book goes live. It catches the mistakes my creative brain refuses to see, and it makes my editors' jobs easier — which means faster turnaround and cleaner final manuscripts.

## What "Quality Control" Actually Means for a Romance Novel

QC isn't copyediting. Copyediting fixes commas and typos.

QC checks the *machine* of the novel:
- Does the timeline make sense?
- Does the evidence or clues pay off?
- Are the characters consistent across 90,000 words?
- Does the romance escalate, or does it repeat the same beat?
- Are the heat scenes earning their place?
- Is the ending satisfying *because* of what was set up?

If copyediting is washing the car, QC is checking that the engine runs.

## My Pre-Publish Audit Stack

I run three audits at different stages of the manuscript. Each one has a specific job and a specific time when it's most useful.

### 1. The Continuity Audit (Every 4–6 Chapters)

This is the most boring audit, and also the one that saves me the most embarrassment. I look at:

- **Timeline.** Does the timeline actually work? If Chapter 3 says it's Tuesday, and Chapter 5 says "three days later," what day is it now?
- **POV consistency.** Am I staying inside one character's head per scene?
- **Artifact tracking.** Every clue, document, object, or piece of evidence gets logged. If a character uses something in Chapter 18, I need to know it was introduced before.
- **Who knows what.** This is the big one. If Heroine discovers a secret in Chapter 10, I need to track who else knows by Chapter 20, who is lying about knowing, and what the reader knows that the characters don't.
- **Canon lock.** In a series, every established rule from previous books still applies. No one gets to break the world just because it's convenient.

I catch a lot here. Once, I had a villain's alibi depend on a meeting that happened *after* the crime. I caught it in this audit. It would have destroyed the entire mystery thread.

### 2. The Romance & Heat Audit (Once per Act)

Romance readers will forgive a lot. They will not forgive chemistry that fizzles, intimacy that feels mechanical, or a couple who falls in love because the plot needs them to.

Every act, I audit the central couple using these questions:

- What does each character want externally in every scene?
- What do they want emotionally but can't admit?
- How does power shift between them?
- What trust is built or destroyed?
- What desire is revealed?
- How does the romance affect the external plot?
- Are their wounds still active, or did they conveniently heal?
- Is this scene repeating an earlier beat?

For heat scenes, I score them on ten criteria: consent clarity, character specificity, emotional charge, erotic vividness, plot integration, power shift, consequence, distinction from previous scenes, POV depth, and prose quality. Anything below a 7 gets rewritten.

This audit is why my readers tell me the steamy scenes feel *earned*. Because they are.

### 3. The Full Manuscript QC (After Draft Completion)

This is the final diagnostic. I score the manuscript across 16 categories from 1 to 10:

1. Opening hook
2. Reader promise delivery
3. Couple chemistry
4. Romance progression
5. Suspense logic
6. Evidence chain
7. Antagonist credibility
8. Midpoint strength
9. Dark turn strength
10. Climax satisfaction
11. Heat scene integration
12. Series continuity
13. POV voice distinction
14. Pacing
15. KDP commercial appeal
16. Anti-generated-prose quality

Anything below a 7 gets a specific fix plan. I don't just flag "pacing is weak." I name the chapters, identify the dead weight, and decide whether to cut, compress, or escalate.

The result is a revision priority list. I tackle the highest-leverage items first, not the easiest ones.

## The Anti-AI Pass Is Part of QC

I use AI heavily in drafting. That means an extra pass is non-negotiable.

The anti-AI pass hunts for:
- Generic metaphors ("his eyes were like dark pools")
- Empty emotional summaries ("she felt a wave of sadness")
- Repetitive internal monologue
- Corporate language without consequence
- Smooth sentences that say nothing
- Filler transitions
- Over-explained dialogue

I replace them with specific action, sharper subtext, sensory detail, and dialogue that reveals character instead of explaining it.

The goal isn't to sound "not AI." It's to sound *human*.

## The Tools Are Simple

I don't use expensive software for this. My stack is:
- A tracking spreadsheet for continuity
- Chapter beat cards in Scrivener
- A checklist for each audit
- A simple scoring rubric for the final QC

The system matters more than the tool. You could run this in a notebook if you were disciplined about it.

## The Real Secret

The real secret is that quality control creates *confidence*. When I hit publish now, I know the timeline works. I know the clues pay off. I know the romance earns its ending. I know no one's eye color changed.

That confidence lets me market harder, launch faster, and sleep better.

Readers can feel it too. They don't know you ran an audit. They just know the book doesn't fall apart in the final act.

## Want to Try This?

Start with one audit. Pick the one that scares you most — usually continuity or romance progression. Run it on your work-in-progress. Fix the three biggest problems. Then run it again next month.

Small, consistent quality checks beat a frantic panic edit two days before launch.
